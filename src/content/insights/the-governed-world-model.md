The most ambitious research programs in AI now share a destination: the **world model**. Instead of predicting the next token, the systems being built learn compressed, predictive models of how the world evolves — from video, from simulation, from embodied interaction. The bet is that intelligence needs a model of the world, not just a model of language.

The bet is right. The enterprise conclusion usually drawn from it is not.

## What a learned world model knows

A learned world model is **implicit, statistical, and predictive**. It answers questions of the form *what is likely to happen next?* — and within that envelope it is remarkable. But everything it knows, it knows the way a distribution knows: without provenance, without authority, without versions, without anyone accountable for its content.

Ask it the questions an organization runs on and the envelope tears:

- Which definition of this service is **in effect** — not probable, in effect?
- Which obligation binds this system in this jurisdiction, **since when**, decided **by whom**?
- What is this change **permitted** to touch, and what must never follow from it?

These are not prediction questions. They are questions of **standing** — and standing cannot be learned from data, because it is not in the data. It is conferred by governance.

## What a governed world model is

A **governed world model** is the complement: **explicit, causal, and declared**. This is what the [Generative System Model](/solutions/gsm) specifies. Every entity in the world being modeled is a *definition* — typed by an Archetype whose schema carries its meaning, versioned as governed snapshots, moved through an explicit lifecycle from draft to approval to activation to retirement. Obligations are not latent regularities; they are Directives operationalized by evaluable Norms, each traceable to the authority that issued it. Causality is not inferred; it is modeled — mechanisms, interactions, effectors, receptors, declared and typed.

Where a learned world model compresses *how the world tends to behave*, the governed world model states *what the organization holds true, requires, and permits* — and can prove who said so, and when.

## Complementary by construction

These are not rivals; they occupy different positions in the same loop. The learned model **proposes**: it predicts, drafts, infers, explores. The governed model **disposes**: it is the context proposals are made against and the boundary that decides what may become action — the [full Poesis position](/insights/governance-owns-the-concerns/) applied to models of the world. Generative systems get their power from the first and their trustworthiness from the second, and the argument that better context beats bigger models — made in [Better context beats bigger models](/insights/why-poesis-matters-for-language-models/) — is exactly the argument for giving learned intelligence a governed world model to operate on.

There is also a directional difference worth naming. A learned world model is built *from* the world, after the fact. A governed world model is built *for* the world — the definition comes first and generates what runs, the inversion set out in [The generative inversion](/insights/the-generative-inversion/). One describes; the other defines. An enterprise needs both, but only one of them can carry its concerns.

The world-model race will produce extraordinary predictors. None of them will ever be able to tell you what your organization has decided. That model you have to declare — and govern.
