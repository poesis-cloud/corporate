A consensus formed this year: as AI agents gain authority, **governance becomes the primary constraint**. Regulators are publishing notes on agentic AI, consultancies are shipping agentic-GRC frameworks, research journals are proposing governance profiles for agents. The diagnosis is right — and almost everything offered under it shares one weakness.

It produces documents.

## The tempo problem

A policy PDF cannot intercept an action. A review board meets at human tempo while agents act at machine tempo; a quarterly audit examines a fleet that has redeployed itself a thousand times since the last one. Governance that describes obligations but cannot evaluate them at the moment of action is not governing agents — it is narrating them.

The requirement, stated plainly: **governance must run at the tempo of the governed.** For human organizations that tempo was meetings. For agentic organizations it is execution.

## What executing governance means

Four properties, each mechanical, none optional:

1. **Obligations are evaluable definitions, not prose.** In [GSM](/solutions/gsm) terms: a Directive opens the governance scope — who governs what, along which dimension — and Norms operationalize it as measurable assertions. The obligation *is* the artifact; there is no translation step where intent gets lost between the policy document and the enforcement config.
2. **Verdicts are deterministic and reproducible.** The [Operator](/solutions/sie/products/operator) evaluates Norms in a sandboxed rule runtime with no ambient authority: the same definitions and the same inputs always produce the same verdict. Every judgment can be replayed and audited — which is what makes delegating authority to agents defensible in front of a regulator at all.
3. **Execution rights are governed facts.** Only definitions the lifecycle has activated can execute, and only where the model explicitly wires them. Nothing runs because a server's configuration happens to say so — what an agent may trigger is itself a versioned, approved statement in the model.
4. **Every action is accountable to an owner.** Concerns are owned by human stakeholders and carried by governance — the [full Poesis position](/insights/governance-owns-the-concerns/). The agent proposes; the stack disposes deterministically where the matter is decidable, and a human disposes where accountability requires it.

## Two tempos, one model

Governing agents turns out to be two problems wearing one name. The first is **conduct**: what an agent may do, step by step, inside a delivery process — the province of the [agentic harness](/insights/what-is-an-agentic-harness/), which resolves, checks, validates, and journals every step from persisted state. The second is **consequence**: what the systems agents act on must remain true, whatever touched them — the province of the governed model itself, evaluated continuously rather than reconstructed at audit time.

Most agentic-governance proposals address conduct with process documents and consequence with dashboards. The Poesis stack addresses both from the same governed definitions — one model, two enforcement tempos.

It is also worth separating this from **model governance**. Model cards, capability evaluations, and AI-Act conformance govern the *model as an artifact* — necessary, and not this. Agentic AI governance governs *actions in your systems*: what is being changed, under which obligation, with whose authority. You can deploy a perfectly documented model and still have entirely ungoverned agents.

## The constraint is the enabler

"Governance becomes the primary constraint" sounds like bad news for agentic ambition. It is the opposite — for whoever makes governance execute. Authority can only be delegated as far as it can be checked; agents get exactly as much autonomy as your governance can evaluate at their tempo. Governance that executes is not the brake on the agentic era. It is the license for it.
